# Web UI Design for the Human Eye — Extraction Notes

## PDF Metadata
- **Title:** Web UI Design for the Human Eye: Content Patterns & Typography
- **Publisher:** UXPin Inc., 2015
- **Authors:** Jerry Cao (lead); Kamil Zieba, Krzysztof Stryjewski, Matt Ellis
- **Page count:** 72 pages (5 chapters)

## Core Theses
1. Content is the foundation, not afterthought. Layouts built around real content.
2. Users SCAN, don't read. F-pattern for content; Z-pattern for action.
3. Typography is a second language (literal + emotional meaning).
4. Visual hierarchy requires ≥3 levels (primary, secondary, tertiary).
5. Legibility ≠ readability — both must be balanced; clarity wins.
6. Eye-attraction governed by contrast: biggest/brightest/warmest/most-isolated wins.

## Content-First Principles

### 1. Content-First Design (p. 9-18)
> "Design in the absence of content is not design, it's decoration." — Zeldman

Develop real content before/alongside visual design. Lorem ipsum only at earliest brainstorming.

### 2. Proto-content Beats Placeholder (p. 15-17)
Rough real text surfaces design issues lorem ipsum hides (long titles, awkward formats).

## Scanning Patterns

### 3. F-Pattern for Content-Heavy Pages (p. 19-28)
Users scan in F or E shape: horizontal across top, vertical down left, secondary horizontals. Users read only **20% of words**.

**Application:** Blog index/archive — title, category, thumbnail along top and left. Strongest content in first two rows.

### 4. The Upper-Left Anchor (p. 21, 27, 37)
Upper-left is the only spot "practically guaranteed to get noticed." Logo/identity goes here. **Never displace it.**

### 5. End-of-Row CTAs (p. 23, 27)
Points 2 and 4 of F-pattern are pause points — ideal for CTAs and ads. The eye pauses before dropping to next row.

### 6. Break Monotony Every ~1000 Pixels (p. 22, 27-28)
Insert "awkward" row to disrupt rhythm — pull-quote, different card width, featured post, divider with icon.

### 7. Right-Sidebar as Secondary Channel (p. 24)
Two roles: (a) related-but-unrelated content (ads, social, related posts) and (b) search/tags/category tools. **Never the article's primary CTA.**

### 8. Z-Pattern for Sparse Action-Oriented Pages (p. 34-40)
Top-left → top-right → diagonal down-left → bottom-right (main CTA lives here).

**F organizes content. Z emphasizes CTA.**

### 9. Z-Pattern Center Holds Hero (p. 37)
Single arresting image keeps interest while pushing eye downward.

### 10. Z-Pattern Backgrounds Stay Muted (p. 38)
Flashy backgrounds distract the eye off-course. Neutral surfaces.

### 11. Repeatable Z (Stacked Z) (p. 39)
Vertical Z stacking (Evernote/Dropbox model) for multiple value propositions, each ending in "Learn More."

### 12. F vs Z by Page Goal (p. 41)
F = organize many items. Z = one specific action via visual narrative. Don't mix without intent.

### 13. Biggest and Brightest Wins (p. 41, 67)
Whatever you make biggest/brightest = first seen, regardless of intent.

## Typography Principles

### 14. Typography Communicates Two Meanings (p. 47-49)
Literal definition + emotional message from visual treatment.

### 15. Match Typography to Audience and Message (p. 50-55)
Same font reads "edgy" to one audience, "classy" to another.

### 16. Same Font, Different Treatment, Different Voice (p. 53-55)
TD Bank example: cautious on homepage, friendly on microsite.

**Don't need many typefaces — need disciplined treatment of small set.**

### 17. The Five-Word Test (p. 52)
List top 5 words that come to mind looking at your typography. If they don't match audience+objective, iterate.

### 18. Don't Be Too Literal (p. 55-56)
"Teen Vogue" doesn't use tween-y font. Coding blog shouldn't use "hacker" pixel font. Restraint preserves credibility.

### 19. Legibility vs Readability (p. 56-60)
- **Legibility** = letter design (strokes, serifs, x-height)
- **Readability** = whole context (color, leading, tracking, length)

**Prioritize clarity over style.**

### 20. Sleek Crisp Typefaces Reduce Cognitive Load (p. 57)
Ornamental type forces decoding tax on every word.

### 21. Typography Should Not Compete With Content (p. 58)
When users notice font more than words, design failed.

### 22. Match Typeface to Role — Pairing (p. 59)
Expressive display (e.g., Knockout) for headlines + highly readable (e.g., Tisa, Proxima Nova) for body.

### 23. Measure: 60-70 Characters (p. 59) — HARD RULE
Line length too wide = readers lose place between lines.

### 24. Line Height 1.3-1.5 (p. 60) — HARD RULE
Tighter = lines crash. Looser = lines disconnect.

## Hierarchy

### 25. Three Levels of Hierarchy (Minimum) (p. 62-64)
- **Primary**: headlines, biggest/brightest/sparse
- **Secondary**: subheads, captions, pull quotes
- **Tertiary**: body content, understated
- Optional **emphasis** layer: italics, bold, links

### 26. Tertiary Body Is Plain on Purpose (p. 63)
Body's goal = immersion, not attraction. Single weight, single color, restrained leading.

### 27. Hierarchy Levers (p. 65-69)
Size, Weight, Italics, Capitalization, Color, Contrast, Space, Position, Orientation, Texture.

### 28. Warm Colors Attract More Than Cool (p. 67)
Warm = red/orange/yellow. Saturated > muted. Warm on cool = max attention.

**One warm accent reserved for CTAs/links. Cool/neutral surroundings.**

### 29. CAPITALIZATION = Yelling (p. 67)
Use sparingly: eyebrow labels, section overlines, tag chips. Never in body or headlines.

### 30. White Space as Hierarchy Tool (p. 68)
Tight space makes text feel cramped/smaller. Generous space makes text feel larger/readable.

### 31. Proximity Conveys Meaning (p. 68) — Gestalt
Proximity = belonging. Group post meta tightly near title. Separate "related posts" with large gap.

### 32. Orientation as Spotlight (p. 68)
Rotated/tilted/vertical short words snap attention. Powerful but easy to overdo.

### 33. Texture by Breaking Patterns (p. 69)
Pull-quotes, blockquotes, callouts, inline code add controlled texture.

### 34. Words Have Length, Sound, Shape (p. 56)
Beyond meaning — length affects display, sound affects reading, shape affects silhouette.

### 35. Bold for Emphasis (p. 66)
Most straightforward; works especially well in tertiary/body type. Reserve `<strong>` for genuine emphasis.

### 36. Italics for Soft Emphasis (p. 66)
Less dramatic than bold. Stack: italic for nuance, bold for force.

2. "Words are the foundation of all interaction." (p. 11, 14)

4. "The F-pattern organizes content, the Z-pattern emphasizes calls-to-action." (p. 35)
5. "Every printed word can communicate two separate meanings: literal definition and emotion." (p. 47-48)
6. "Unless you want to be unreadable, prioritize clarity over style." (p. 60)
7. "Your user's eye will always be attracted to the biggest and brightest elements on a page." (p. 41)
