# Clever Interactive Techniques for Web Storytelling — Extraction Notes

## PDF Metadata
- **Title:** Clever Interactive Techniques for Web Storytelling
- **Authors:** Jerry Cao & Cameron Chapman, UXPin Inc., 2015
- **Page count:** 27 pages

## Core Theses
1. Interactivity is optional, but engagement is mandatory.
2. Match interactive complexity to story complexity — no more, no less.
3. Interaction design is not ornamental — it converts observers to participants.
4. Every interactive story needs a narrative arc (beginning, middle, end) from user's POV.
5. Effects must add value, not impress.
6. Know your users before designing playful/hidden interactions.

## Principles

### 1. Story-Sized Fidelity (p. 7)
Tell the story in as many or as few visuals as it needs. Wikipedia ≠ Tesla.

### 2. Interactivity Is Context-Dependent (p. 7)
Reference sites: hurt by interactive. Narrative/persuasion sites: helped by interactive.

### 3. Make the User a Character (p. 8-10)
Personalize via input (role, choices) or real-time data. Slavery Footprint questionnaire, Tesla "How do I take a road trip?"

### 4. Balance Product and User Focus (p. 10)
Frame product questions as user concerns. Tesla example.

### 5. Tasteful Parallax for Linear Journeys (p. 10, 16)
Immersive linear narrative — but "tasteful" matters. Reserve for flagship/hero, not standard articles.

### 6. Gamification = Cue → Routine → Reward (p. 11-13)
Habit loop. Cues hard to change; iterate on routine and reward.

### 7. Right-Size Rewards (p. 12)
Too small = no incentive. Too large = "there must be a catch."

### 8. Visual Reward Loops (p. 12)
"Visuals that change, improve, or do something cool" are valid rewards. Subtle copy-confirmation animation, completion checkmark, filled progress ring.

### 9. Voluntary-Reward Quest Pattern (p. 13-14)
Dropbox 7-step checklist for 250MB. Taps human accomplishment need.

### 10. Choose-Your-Own-Adventure Framing (p. 14)
Different rewards for different actions. Branching paths increase ownership.

### 11. Supplement Common Actions (p. 15)
Clicking and scrolling are mindless — add value, don't invent new gestures. Hover previews, viewport entry animations.

### 12. Image-Copy Tone Consistency (p. 15)
Serious engineering blog ≠ cartoon mascots next to formal titles.

### 13. Hero Headline Rule: 5-7 Words (p. 16) — HARD RULE
Hero zone is for instantaneous read.

### 14. Micro-Parallax (p. 16-17)
You don't need full parallax. Small rotate-in-place or hover-reveal works.

### 15. Animation as Pacing Tool (p. 17-18)
Stagger reveal of grid items controls pace. Without it, simultaneous overload.

### 16. Restraint: Not Every Corner Needs Animation (p. 18)
"Effects compound; restraint amplifies them." Pick 3-4 signature interactions.

### 17. Smooth Responsive Visuals (p. 19-20)
React to scroll, clicks, form. Babel app's tumbling cat that rights itself at pause.

### 18. Easter Eggs for Discoverability Joy (p. 21-22)
Only appropriate for: game sites, contest sites, technically proficient audiences, fun-premise sites. **Dev/portfolio audience qualifies.**

### 19. Signal That Hidden Interactions Exist (p. 22)
"Click around to find hidden interactions!" Hint or onboarding tooltip.

### 20. Prioritize Visibility by Task Criticality (p. 23)
Critical actions visible. Playful ones can hide. Core nav always visible; secondary delights discoverable but not load-bearing.

### 21. Beginning, Middle, End — User's POV (p. 24-25)
Map stimulus → engagement → goal. Site may only own part of journey.

### 22. Localized Experience Maps (p. 25)
High-level customer journey first, then localized map for your site's segment, then align multimedia.

### 23. Multimedia in Service of Narrative (p. 25)
"Which beat of the narrative does this serve?" Required editorial check.

## Key Quotes
1. "Not every visual story has to be interactive! Your visual story does not have to be interactive to be effective!" (p. 6)
2. "It's all about making your story as detailed or simple as it needs to be: no more, no less." (p. 7)
3. "Embed additional interactivity into existing user actions, and you'll find that the effects quickly add up to create a more delightful experience." (p. 18)
4. "Actions that are critical to completing user goals must be explicitly visible, while those that are more occasional can afford to be less visible or even hidden." (p. 23)
5. "Don't add interactive elements just for the sake of adding them." (p. 26)
