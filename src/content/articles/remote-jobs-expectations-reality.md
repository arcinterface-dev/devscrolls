## 1. Why I Started Looking at Remote Jobs

The reasons were straightforward. Like many Indian developers, I am staying away from my hometown — in a completely different state — because that is where the jobs are. That means paying rent for a separate flat, daily food expenses, and travel costs every time I want to see my family. Add it all up and a significant chunk of my salary goes toward just sustaining the fact that I live away from home for work.

I had some early exposure to remote work at the start of my career — a part-time engagement, roughly 20 hours per week. It was enough to show me what working from home could look like. The ability to work from my hometown, cut the flat rent and food expenses entirely, save significantly more, and actually stay with my family — that was the real pull.

And then there is the compensation gap. Indian developers working for US or European companies remotely can earn 2x to 4x their local market rate. For a senior developer, that difference can be significant enough to change your financial trajectory entirely.

So I started applying.

## 2. The Application and Interview Reality

Let me be upfront about this: getting noticed is hard. You are competing globally — against developers from Eastern Europe, Latin America, Southeast Asia, and everywhere else. These are not local walk-in interviews where you show up and get a shot. Every remote listing attracts hundreds of applicants, and the spray-and-pray strategy of mass-applying does not work.

What actually worked for me was building a visible presence. I structured my LinkedIn profile specifically for the roles I was targeting. I built a portfolio with real projects — not tutorial clones, but tools and applications that demonstrated how I think about architecture and user experience. And most importantly, I connected directly with the hiring managers and HRs for positions I was genuinely interested in, rather than just clicking "Apply" and hoping.

The shortlisting process is slower too. Do not expect a call within two days like you might with local companies. Remote-first companies often have async hiring pipelines where your application moves through multiple stages over weeks.

## 3. How Remote Interviews Are Different

Some remote interviews follow the same structure as traditional office interviews — a technical round, a system design discussion, and a culture fit call. But many others are different in ways I did not expect.

Several companies sent me a take-home project first. One asked me to build a dashboard from scratch and then explain my architectural decisions in a follow-up call. The focus was less on "can you answer this question correctly" and more on "how do you approach a problem when nobody is watching."

The hardest part was communication. Remote teams run on async updates, documented decisions, and timezone-aware collaboration. If the company is based in the US, your standup might be at 9 PM IST. If something urgent comes up, you might get a message at 1 AM. This is not a bug in the system — it is the system. Companies actively evaluate whether you can handle this during the interview process itself.

Real-time communication skills matter more than I expected. In an office, you can walk over to someone's desk and ask a quick question. In a remote setup, you write a clear message, attach context, and wait. If your communication is vague, you lose hours to back-and-forth. Some interviewers test this by giving you an ambiguous requirement and seeing how you ask clarifying questions.

## 4. The PF Fear That Almost Stopped Me

This is the section I wish someone had written before I started exploring remote roles.

I received a legitimate offer from a foreign company. The role was good, the compensation was significantly higher than my local salary, and the work was interesting. But the HR mentioned something that triggered an immediate red flag: they do not provide Indian government benefits like EPF (Employees' Provident Fund), Gratuity, or ESI (Employees' State Insurance).

My first instinct was to walk away.

In India, having a Provident Fund account and a Universal Account Number (UAN) is considered a standard marker of legitimate employment. Every formal employer deducts PF, and your UAN builds a government-verified trail of your entire career history. The common fear — and I had this exact fear — is that if you take a job without PF, your future Background Verification (BGV) checks at traditional IT companies will hit a roadblock. They will see no PF record for that period and assume your work history cannot be verified.

Here is the uncomfortable truth: **that fear is not entirely irrational.**

Even at many MNCs, the very first question their HR asks during the onboarding process is: *"Do you have your previous company's PF/UAN number, employment record, and all past companies' joining letters, relieving letters, and payslips?"* These are treated as mandatory documents — not optional extras. I have seen people face serious friction during their onboarding process at well-known companies simply because they could not produce a complete set of these documents. Without them, it becomes very hard to get through the door.

The reason is simple: India has a massive fake experience problem. Because unverified resumes are so common, HR teams use PF/UAN records as a tamper-proof government-backed verification method. If you tell them "I don't have a PF record for my last role," many recruiters will drop you before you even get to the technical round. They are not being unreasonable — they are protecting themselves against unverified experience claims that the industry deals with every day.

This is the reality that scared me, and it will probably scare you too.

## 5. What I Should Have Done Instead

After walking away from that offer and spending weeks researching how global remote hiring actually works, I realized I made a mistake. Here is what I did not understand at the time.

### Why global remote jobs cannot offer PF

When a foreign company does not have a registered legal entity in India, they cannot open an Indian EPF or ESI account for you. It is not that they are trying to deny you benefits — they literally cannot comply with Indian labor laws without a local entity. 

Instead, they hire you as an **Independent Contractor** or Consultant. This is the standard legal framework for cross-border remote work. Some companies choose to go through an **Employer of Record (EOR)** — services like Deel, Multiplier, or Oyster that act as your legal employer in India and handle PF, taxes, and compliance on behalf of the foreign company. But many startups and mid-sized global companies skip the EOR route because it adds $400 to $700+ per month in fees per employee.

### How to handle the HR conversation

If a future employer asks for your PF details and you do not have them from your remote contract period, you do not just say "I don't have PF." That triggers the fake experience alarm in the recruiter's head.

Instead, you control the narrative: *"I was working as an independent contractor for a foreign entity that does not have a registered presence in India, so Indian PF does not apply to that engagement. However, I have my Form 26AS showing the inward remittances, my bank statements with regular salary credits, my signed contractor agreement, and my relieving letter. These documents clear standard Background Verification."*

Most sensible recruiters at modern product companies and global startups understand this model perfectly. They see it regularly. The friction is mostly at traditional IT service companies where the BGV process is more rigid.

### What you need to keep as proof

To pass a BGV without PF records, maintain a clean paper trail:

- **Bank statements** highlighting regular salary credits from the foreign company
- **Tax records** — your Form 26AS, Annual Information Statement (AIS), or ITR filings showing the inward remittances
- **Signed contractor agreement** and monthly invoices
- **Relieving letter** or end-of-contract confirmation
- **Manager or HR reference** from the company who can verify your tenure via an official corporate email

Keep this documentation organized from day one. If you start collecting it after you have already left, it becomes much harder.

## 6. The Financial Trade-Offs You Must Know

Working as a contractor shifts the entire financial responsibility onto your shoulders. The higher pay is real, but it comes with trade-offs you need to plan for before accepting the offer.

### You must build your own safety net

As a contractor, you lose the employer's 12% PF contribution match and the five-year gratuity payout. You will not have ESI or corporate health insurance coverage. You need to proactively open your own **Public Provident Fund (PPF)** or **National Pension System (NPS)** account to handle retirement savings. And you need to purchase comprehensive health insurance independently.

Nobody will do this for you. If you are not disciplined about it, you will earn more and save less.

### The taxation can actually work in your favor

As a contractor, your income is classified as professional fees rather than a standard salary. This opens up **Section 44ADA** — a presumptive taxation scheme that allows eligible professionals (including IT and software consultancy) to declare only 50% of their gross receipts as taxable income. The remaining 50% is treated as business expenses, which can significantly lower your tax burden.

There are limits to be aware of: Section 44ADA applies to gross receipts up to **₹50 Lakhs per year** (or ₹75 Lakhs if your cash receipts are under 5% of total receipts). If your income exceeds this, you will need to maintain full books of accounts and get a tax audit done.

One more thing: if your annual receipts cross **₹20 Lakhs**, you are required to register for GST. In special category states, the threshold is ₹10 Lakhs.

> **Disclaimer:** I am sharing my understanding from personal research. Tax laws change, and individual circumstances vary. Consult a Chartered Accountant before making financial decisions based on this information.

### The job security question

This is the trade-off that nobody talks about openly. Most global remote contractor roles are not permanent in the way an Indian FTE position is. Your contract might be for 6 months, 1 year, or 2 years. When the project ends or the company downsizes, you are back to job hunting. There is no severance, no notice period protection under Indian labor law, and no unemployment insurance.

Global remote jobs usually offer significantly higher base pay specifically to compensate for this instability. But you need to mentally budget for gaps between contracts and save accordingly.

## 7. Red Flags to Watch Out For

Not every remote job listing is legitimate. Here are warning signs I have noticed during my exploration:

- **"Full-time remote" with mandatory 9-to-5 in a foreign timezone.** If a US company expects you online from 9 PM to 5 AM IST every day, that is not flexibility — that is a night shift without calling it one.
- **No contract or vague terms.** A legitimate company will always provide a clear contractor agreement or EOR employment contract before you start. If they want you to begin work on "trust," walk away.
- **Payment through informal channels.** Salary should come through wire transfer, Payoneer, or a recognized payment platform — not cryptocurrency or peer-to-peer apps with no paper trail.
- **Unusually low rates for "remote."** If a "global company" is offering local Indian rates, they are not hiring remote talent for your expertise — they are arbitraging your labor costs.

## Conclusion

I initially walked away from a remote opportunity out of fear. That fear was not baseless — the PF/BGV friction in traditional Indian hiring is real. But it is a manageable problem, not a career-ending one.

If the company is legitimate, the pay is good, and you are disciplined enough to manage your own taxes, investments, and retirement savings, saying yes to a global remote contractor role can be a significant step up in your career.

The key is going in with your eyes open. Know the legal framework, keep your paperwork organized, and do not let the fear of a future background check scare you away from a real opportunity.
